**Checkpoint:** none, this part continues in the `functional` demo database

## Why this matters

You do not need to be an accountant to work on Odoo. You do need to stop being surprised
by accounting, because a large share of real tickets touch it: an invoice with the wrong
tax, a report that does not tie, a payment that will not reconcile, a month that will not
close.

There is exactly one idea to internalise, and it is this chapter's title. **An invoice
and a journal entry are the same model.** Not similar, not related by a foreign key: the
same table, the same class, told apart by one field.

Put plainly: Odoo keeps one notebook for everything involving money, and an invoice is
simply a page in it that Odoo draws nicely. Every developer who has not internalised that
eventually goes hunting for an invoice model, does not find one, and ends up writing code
that treats invoices as a special case, then cannot explain why the accounting is wrong.

So this chapter does it in the honest order. First you hand-write a journal entry, which
forces you to meet debits and credits with nothing else in the way. Then you go and look
at the invoice chapter 22 produced, and find it is built from exactly the same parts.

## Concepts

Four ideas, and they build on each other. First the one rule double-entry actually has,
then the three models that implement it, then the single field that makes an invoice and a
journal entry the same thing, and finally what happens when a period is closed for good.
If you only keep one, keep the third.

### Double-entry, in the only two sentences you need

Every transaction is recorded at least twice: once as a <Term k="debit / credit">**debit**</Term>, once as a **credit**,
and the two totals must be equal. That is the whole rule.

What debits and credits *mean* depends on the account. Forget any sense of good and bad;
the rule is purely positional:

- A **debit** increases an asset or an expense, and decreases a liability, equity or
  income.
- A **credit** does the reverse.

Those five words are the whole vocabulary, so here they are in plain terms: an **asset** is
something you own, a **liability** is something you owe, an **expense** is money spent, and
**income** is money earned. (**Equity** is what would be left for the owners after paying
every liability, and it barely appears in this chapter.)

So buying a 250.00 tool cabinet with money from the bank is a debit of 250.00 to an
equipment account (an expense went up) and a credit of 250.00 to the bank account (an
asset went down).

Nothing was created or destroyed. Value moved, and the entry records both ends. It is the
same shape as chapter 25's stock moves, where goods always come *from* one location and go
*to* another. Accounting does it with money.

Odoo does not treat balance as a convention you should respect. It **refuses to post an
unbalanced entry**, which you will see for yourself.

### The three models

Odoo needs only three tables to hold everything above: a list of buckets, a book to write
in, and the entries themselves. Here is how they hang together, and the 250.00 cabinet
from a moment ago is the example running through it.

<Mermaid
  label="The three accounting models: a journal owns a move, a move holds balanced debit and credit lines, and each line points at an account in the chart of accounts"
  chart={`flowchart TD
    J["account.journal<br/>Sales, Purchases, Bank, Misc"]
    M["account.move<br/>one document<br/>(move_type says which kind)"]
    L1["account.move.line<br/>debit 250.00<br/>account: Purchase of Equipments"]
    L2["account.move.line<br/>credit 250.00<br/>account: Bank"]
    A["account.account<br/>the chart of accounts"]
    J --> M
    M --> L1
    M --> L2
    L1 -.-> A
    L2 -.-> A`}
/>

- The <Term k="chart of accounts (`account.account`)">`account.account`</Term> is the chart of accounts: the list of buckets value can sit in.
  Each carries an `account_type` (`asset_receivable`, `asset_cash`, `expense`, `income`,
  `equity`, and so on), and that type is what reports group by, which is how a report can
  say "assets" without hard-coding account numbers. Your demo database ships a generic
  chart with 55 accounts visible to this company. Only *this* company: the same
  multi-company visibility rule that the Gotchas section below shows journals obeying.
- A <Term k="journal (`account.journal`)">`account.journal`</Term> is the book an entry is written in, and it owns the numbering
  sequence. Sales, Purchases, Bank and Miscellaneous are the ones you will meet first.
- **<Term k="account.move / account.move.line">`account.move`</Term>** is one accounting document, and **`account.move.line`** are its
  debit and credit lines. The lines are where the money actually is.

### The duality, stated plainly

Before the field names, the picture. Odoo keeps **one notebook** for everything involving
money, and every money event gets **one page** in it. A customer invoice is a page. A bill
from a supplier is a page. A hand-written correction is a page. Each page carries a label
at the top saying which kind it is, and underneath the label every page has the identical
structure: money-in on one side, money-out on the other, the two sides equal.

That label is the *only* difference between them. It is worth being blunt about why this
surprises people. Open an invoice in Odoo and it looks like its own special thing: a
customer name, a due date, a Send button, a PDF. It is not a special thing. It is an
ordinary notebook page, and Odoo read the label, decided "this one is an invoice", and drew
it prettier.

The label is a field called `move_type`, and this is its entire selection:

| `move_type` | What it is |
|---|---|
| `entry` | Journal Entry (a plain, hand-written or machine-written entry) |
| `out_invoice` | Customer Invoice |
| `out_refund` | Customer Credit Note |
| `in_invoice` | Vendor Bill |
| `in_refund` | Vendor Credit Note |
| `out_receipt` | Sales Receipt |
| `in_receipt` | Purchase Receipt |

There is no `account.invoice` model. There has not been one for years. An invoice is an
`account.move` whose `move_type` is `out_invoice`, with the same balanced
`account.move.line` children as any other entry, plus some invoice-flavoured fields on
top. Its "invoice lines" and its "journal items" are the same records looked at through
two different views.

Once you see it this way, a lot of Odoo stops being surprising: why invoices balance, why
posting an invoice affects reports immediately, why you can find an invoice's accounting
impact without leaving the model you started on.

### Draft, posted, and where numbers come from

A move is `draft` until you post it. Two things follow, and both matter:

- **A draft move has no number.** Its `name` is `False`, not a placeholder. The journal's
  sequence is asked for a number at *posting* time.
- That is precisely why accounting numbering has no gaps: a number is never spent on
  something that might be abandoned. It also means numbering is legally defensible, which
  is the point of the whole arrangement.

Chapter 34's sequences are the same mechanism you already met, applied where the stakes
are highest.

### Lock dates: closing a period for good

Posting isn't the last word. A posted entry can still be reset to draft and edited, and
that is fine in the middle of a month. It is not fine once the books for a period are
final and somebody has filed a tax return against them. What you want at that point is a
door that closes behind you.

That door is a **lock date**: a date on the company record, before which Odoo stops
accepting edits. `res.company` carries five of them, confirmed live:

- **`sale_lock_date`** blocks edits to customer invoices and credit notes dated on or
  before it.
- **`purchase_lock_date`** does the same for the vendor side.
- **`fiscalyear_lock_date`** blocks everything: any move, any journal.
- **`hard_lock_date`** (new in Odoo 19) also blocks everything, and is the interesting one.
- **`tax_lock_date`**, labelled **Tax Return Lock Date**, blocks entries *that carry taxes*
  dated on or before it. It is the one you rarely set by hand: Odoo sets it for you when a
  tax closing entry is posted, which is the software saying "this period has been declared,
  stop editing it". This chapter does not exercise it.

Four of those five are "soft" locks. Softness has a precise meaning here: an
`account.lock_exception` record can grant one user a temporary, logged override past any of
them, fiscal year included. So a determined accountant with the right rights can always get
back in.

"Four" is not a judgement call, incidentally. Core keeps the list in a constant called
`SOFT_LOCK_DATE_FIELDS`, and those four are exactly its contents.

`hard_lock_date` is the exception, and what makes it **the strictest of the five**
(verified against `res.company`'s own field definitions in core) is not what it blocks but
that nothing can get around it. Its own help text says so plainly: "irreversible and does
not allow any exception." No model grants an exception against it, for anyone, ever. It is
the one lock on this list you cannot talk your way around later, so it is also the one to
be careful with.

Setting any of them isn't a bare field write, either. Odoo checks the books are actually
clean first, and refuses if they are not:

```text
There are still unreconciled bank statement lines in the period you want to lock.
You should either reconcile or delete them.
```

That's a real, live response to trying to set `fiscalyear_lock_date` on the
`functional` database, not a hypothetical: ten bank statement lines from June
through August were still sitting unreconciled, and Odoo refused to lock a period
that contained them rather than silently letting the lock hide the mess. "A month
that will not close," this chapter's opening line, is precisely this validation
firing for real.

## Hands-on

Continuing in the `functional` database. First you hand-write and post a journal entry,
then you read what posting it actually did, and find the invoice from chapter 22 built

<Callout type="warn" title="Half the accounting menus are missing, on purpose">
  Community's accounting app is called **Invoicing** (the one called Accounting is the
  Enterprise one). More surprising: several menus you will look for are not in it. Chart of
  Accounts, Journal Entries and the accounting reports are gated behind a group called

<Step>
### Try to post an entry that does not balance

Journal entries are the other gated screen, so open them the same way:
`http://localhost:8069/odoo/action-account.action_move_journal_line`

Press **New**, pick the **Miscellaneous Operations** journal, and on the **Journal Items**
tab (the one the callout above unlocked for you) add two lines:

- Account `611000 Purchase of Equipments`, **Label** `Workshop tool cabinet`, **Debit**
  `250.00`
- Account `101401 Bank`, **Label** `Paid from bank`, **Credit** `200.00`

Deliberately 250 against 200, so the entry does not balance. Press **Post**. Odoo stops
you:

```text
The entry is not balanced.
```

![The \"Invalid Operation\" dialog reading The entry is not balanced, after posting a draft with 250.00 debit against 200.00 credit](/screens/ch27-entry-not-balanced.jpg)

That is not a warning you can dismiss. Double-entry is enforced at the database boundary,
not left to your good intentions.

Now read what just happened more carefully, because it is stronger than it looks. That
refusal is **not** a rule about posting. It comes from `_check_balanced`, and if you go
and read it in `account/models/account_move.py` you will find it runs on **create and
write**, not inside `action_post`:

```python
@contextmanager
def _check_balanced(self, container):
    ''' Assert the move is fully balanced debit = credit.
    An error is raised if it's not the case.
    '''
```

So an unbalanced entry cannot be *stored at all*, not even as a draft. Pressing Post is
simply what made the browser try to save first, which is the moment the constraint fired.
Click the save (cloud) icon instead of Post and you get the identical refusal a moment
earlier. Prove it from the shell if you like, where there is no form in the way:

```python
>>> J = env["account.journal"].search([("code", "=", "MISC")], limit=1)
>>> exp = env["account.account"].search([("code", "=", "611000")], limit=1)
>>> bank = env["account.account"].search([("code", "=", "101401")], limit=1)
>>> env["account.move"].create({
...     "journal_id": J.id,
...     "move_type": "entry",
...     "line_ids": [
...         (0, 0, {"account_id": exp.id,  "name": "x", "debit": 250.0, "credit": 0.0}),
...         (0, 0, {"account_id": bank.id, "name": "y", "debit": 0.0,   "credit": 200.0}),
...     ],
... })
odoo.exceptions.UserError: The entry is not balanced.
```

`create` never returned a record. Which means the important consequence: **there is no
draft sitting in the database for you to go and inspect.** Nothing was written. That is a
genuinely higher bar than "you may not post this", and it is why you will never find a
half-finished, unbalanced entry in an Odoo database.

</Step>

<Step>
### Balance it, and save without posting

Back in the browser, change the credit to **250.00**, then click **Save** (the cloud icon)
and *stop there*. Do not post it yet: the next step is about the difference between a saved
draft and a posted entry, and you need the draft to exist to see it.

This time the save succeeds, because the entry balances.

![A saved but unposted journal entry: Draft in the statusbar, no entry number, two balanced lines of 250.00 debit and credit](/screens/ch27-entry-saved-draft.jpg)

Now grab a handle on it from the
shell you opened in step 1:

```python
>>> env.cr.rollback()
>>> mv = env["account.move"].search([("line_ids.name", "=", "Workshop tool cabinet")], order="id desc", limit=1)
>>> mv.name, mv.state
(False, 'draft')
```

Two details in that snippet earn their keep. The `env.cr.rollback()` is the stale-snapshot
problem again: your shell has been open since step 1, so it cannot see what the browser
just committed until you refresh it. And the search matches on the **line label** rather
than just the journal, because searching `journal_id.code = MISC` with `order="id desc"`
would happily hand you some *other* old draft that happens to be newest, and you would
then spend the next two steps reading a record you never touched.

</Step>

- You **earned** 750.00. Income went up, so **Product Sales is credited**.

Put the two side by side and there is nothing to tell apart:

| Your hand-written entry | Odoo's invoice |
|---|---|
| Equipment, debit 250.00 | Account Receivable, debit 750.00 |
| Bank, credit 250.00 | Product Sales, credit 750.00 |

Two lines, one debit, one credit, equal amounts, both of them an `account.move`. You wrote
one of these yourself in a form with two boxes; Odoo generated the other one from a sales

`move_type` plus a different form view, and it is the entire difference.

```python
>>> sum(inv.line_ids.mapped('debit')) == sum(inv.line_ids.mapped('credit'))
True
```

**The sentence to carry out of this chapter:** an invoice is an ordinary journal entry that

changing.

## Gotchas

- **A draft move's `name` is `False`, not `"/"` or `""`.** Code that formats
  `move.name` for a draft entry prints `False` into a report, and code that tests
  `if move.name` behaves differently before and after posting. If you need a label for an
  unposted move, use `display_name`.
- **`account.account.code` is computed, not stored, and company-dependent in Odoo 19.**
  Reading it while a different company is active returns `False`, silently. Verified: the
  same line read as the wrong company gives `False` and as the right one gives
  `'400000'`. Use `account.with_company(move.company_id).code`, and be very careful with
  psql queries against a `code` column, because the value lives in
  `account.code.mapping` rather than on the account row.
- **The shell does not apply the company filter the interface does.** This demo database
  has three companies and eighteen journals, but only eleven belong to the active one
  (eleven is the count from step 1; it is seven if you have not yet reached chapter 42 and
  installed `point_of_sale`, which adds four to this company). A
  bare `search([])` in a script crosses company boundaries in a way the same action in
  the interface would not, which is a genuinely dangerous difference when you are writing
  a fix against production data.
- **Do not look for `account.invoice`.** It does not exist. Searching core for it, or
  writing `env["account.invoice"]`, is the clearest sign of a tutorial or answer written
  for Odoo 8 or 9.
- **Unbalanced means unsavable, not merely unpostable, so "my code posted a broken entry"
  is never the bug.** `_check_balanced` is a create/write constraint rather than a check
  inside `action_post`, so an unbalanced move cannot even be stored as a draft. If your
  `create` or `write` raises `The entry is not balanced.`, the arithmetic really is wrong,
  usually because a rounding difference or a tax line was computed by hand rather than
  taken from Odoo. The corollary is useful when debugging: you will never find a
  half-finished unbalanced entry sitting in the database, because there is no way to put
  one there.
- **Setting a broad lock date can fail before it ever touches your target entry.**
  `fiscalyear_lock_date` and `hard_lock_date` both check for unreconciled bank
  statement lines first and refuse to set at all if any exist in the period. That
  refusal is doing its job, not blocking you by mistake.
