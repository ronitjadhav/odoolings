**Goal:** place a real order through the actual online store and read the sale.order
it becomes; open a real POS session, ring up two sales, close the till, and read the
journal entries it posts; then survey the website builder's snippet library, what it
is, and when reaching for it beats writing an OWL component. ·
**Time:** ~1.5 h · **Checkpoint:** none, this chapter runs entirely on the `functional`
database with core Odoo apps, no `librefleet` code changes.

## Why this matters

Chapters 39 to 41 built OWL components by hand: a systray widget, a field, a client
action. That is the right tool when the behaviour is genuinely custom. Most of what a
business needs from its public-facing pages is not custom at all, it is a hero image,
a pricing table, a contact form, arranged and restyled, and Odoo ships that entire job
as a drag-and-drop **snippet** library so nobody has to write a component for it. Knowing
the builder exists, and what it is actually built from, is worth more than a chapter
building yet another widget.

There is a real objection buried in that, and it deserves saying plainly. You have just
spent three chapters learning to build OWL components, and this chapter opens by telling
you that most of the work you would reach for them on does not need them. That can read as
the tutorial undercutting what it just taught you.

It is not. The skill here is the one nobody writes on a job description: recognising which
of the two situations you are in *before* you start typing. A page that needs to **look**
like something is a snippet, and building a component for it is a week spent redoing work
Odoo already shipped. A page that needs to **know** something is a component, and reaching
for a snippet there is a dead end you discover late. Telling those apart is the lesson.

**Point of Sale** earns its place here for a sharper reason: it is Odoo's largest OWL
application by far, and it is offline-first, meaning the till itself is a separate
bootstrap that has to keep ringing up sales through a dropped connection and sync
later. That combination, a real OWL SPA plus a completely different reliability model
from the backend web client, makes it the natural case study to close Part 7 on.

Notice what that implies about how you read the POS half of this chapter: you are not
going to write POS, you are going to open a real one and watch it work. Reading a large
codebase somebody else wrote, well enough to know where your change would go, is a
different skill from writing a small one from scratch, and on a real client project it is
the one that gets used.

This chapter also finally closes the loop Parts 4 and 5 opened: sale, purchase,
stock, manufacturing and accounting all met a customer who placed an order through a
screen. Two screens close it, both covered here. POS is the screen for a customer
standing at a counter, and its session close is where cash and card sales become the
same kind of journal entry chapter 27 taught you to read. The website's shop is the
screen for a customer who never walks in at all, and it produces the exact same
`sale.order` chapter 22 taught you to read, just created by a stranger on the
internet instead of a salesperson at a desk.

## Concepts

### A website page is an `ir.ui.view`, nothing more exotic

```console
>>> page = env["website.page"].search([("url", "=", "/")])
>>> page.view_id.key
'website.homepage'
>>> page.view_id.arch_db[:60]
'<t name="Homepage" t-name="website.homepage">\n    <t t-call...'
```

A page is a `website.page` record pointing at an `ir.ui.view`, and `arch_db` is the
same QWeb you have been reading since chapter 16. There is no second templating
system for the website; it is server-side QWeb, exactly like chapter 36's PDF
reports, rendered as HTML instead of piped through wkhtmltopdf.

### Snippets are QWeb templates with a name registered for the builder

```console
>>> len(env["ir.ui.view"].search([("key", "like", "website.s_")]))
191
```

Every draggable block in the editor is one more `ir.ui.view` under the `website.s_*`
naming convention, the same registration idea chapters 39 to 41 used for the
`fields`, `actions` and `systray` registries, just expressed as a naming convention in
`ir.ui.view` rather than a JS `registry.category(...).add(...)` call. Dragging a
snippet onto a page copies its arch into the page's own `arch_db`; editing it
afterward edits that copy, not the shared template, which is why customizing one
page's hero section never touches any other page using the same snippet.

<Mermaid label="A website page is one ir.ui.view record whose arch is built by copying snippet templates into it; the backend web client and the public website load through two different asset bundles of the same OWL framework." chart={`flowchart LR
    Snippet["ir.ui.view<br/>key: website.s_*"] -->|"dragged onto a page,<br/>arch copied in"| Page["website.page<br/>-> ir.ui.view (the page)"]
    Page --> Render["Rendered as HTML,<br/>same QWeb engine as ch16's views"]
    Backend["Backend web client<br/>web.assets_backend"] -.->|"same OWL framework,<br/>different bundle"| Frontend["Public website<br/>web.assets_frontend"]
`} />

This matters for a detail chapters 39 to 41 did not have to think about: **your OWL
components do not automatically run on the website.** They loaded into
`web.assets_backend`, and the public site boots a different bundle,
`web.assets_frontend`. A systray widget or a field widget has no meaning outside the
backend it was built for.

### The online store is `website_sale`, and it still just makes a `sale.order`

Everything chapter 22 taught about `sale.order` (states, order lines, the quote-to-
order lifecycle) applies unchanged to an order a customer places through the
website. `website_sale` is the app that adds the shop, cart, and checkout flow on
top of `website` and `sale`; installing it is what turns product pages from static
content into something with an **Add to cart** button at all. What it produces is
not a new kind of document, it's the same `sale.order` model, with `website_id` set
and, if a payment provider handled it, a linked `payment.transaction`.

One real wrinkle worth knowing before you check out for the first time: a
manual payment method like Wire Transfer doesn't confirm the order immediately.
The `sale.order` lands in `state = 'sent'` (Quotation Sent) and its
`payment.transaction` sits at `state = 'pending'`, waiting for someone to actually
see the money arrive and confirm it by hand. A real-time gateway (Stripe, PayPal)
would confirm automatically; a bank transfer, by its nature, can't.

### A POS session is a normal Odoo record with an offline app in front of it

`pos.session` is a plain model: `state` moves through `opening_control` → `opened` →
`closing_control` → `closed`, and it links to `pos.order` records the same way any
parent links to its children. What makes POS different is what happens between
"opened" and "closing control": the actual selling runs in a separate SPA
(`/pos/ui/...`), a *single-page application*, meaning one page that loads once and then
redraws itself from then on rather than ever asking the server for another page, backed
by an in-browser database (IndexedDB) so a dropped Wi-Fi
connection does not stop a cashier from ringing up the next customer. Orders queue
locally and sync to the server as connectivity allows, which is the offline-first part
the plan calls out, and the reason POS cannot simply be "one more client action" like
chapter 41's dashboard.

## Hands-on

This chapter runs on `functional` (Parts 4-5's database), not `tutorial`: Point of
Sale and the website builder are core apps you are installing and using directly,
with no `librefleet` code to write.

```console
$ docker compose exec odoo odoo -c /etc/odoo/odoo.conf -d functional -i point_of_sale,website_sale --stop-after-init
```

<Step>
### Place a real order through the online store

Enable a payment method first; the demo data ships every provider disabled. Wire
Transfer needs no external account, real gateway or fake credentials, which makes
it the honest choice for a hands-on:

```console
$ docker compose exec odoo odoo shell -c /etc/odoo/odoo.conf -d functional --no-http
```

```python
>>> wire = env['payment.provider'].search([('code', '=', 'custom'), ('company_id', '=', env.company.id)]).filtered(lambda p: 'Wire' in p.name)
>>> wire.state = 'enabled'
>>> env.cr.commit()
```

Now shop for real: `http://localhost:8069/shop`, pick any product, choose a variant.

![The Customizable Desk product page with Color (White selected) and Legs (Steel selected, Aluminium +$50.40) options, $675.00 price, and an Add to cart button](/screens/ch42-shop-product-variants.jpg)

**Add to cart**, then **Go to Checkout** on the upsell dialog that appears (skip the
cross-sell offers, Conference Chair and Warranty, unless you want to see how they
attach to the same order). Confirm the address chapter's own account already has on
file, pick **Wire Transfer** as the payment method, and **Pay now**:

![The order confirmation page reading "Thank you for your order, Order S00047" with real Wire Transfer bank account details and a $675.00 total](/screens/ch42-shop-order-confirmation.jpg)

That's a real order, `S00047`, not a sandbox mockup.
</Step>

<Step>
### Read what it actually created

```python
>>> order = env['sale.order'].search([('name', '=', 'S00047')])
>>> order.state, order.website_id.name, order.amount_total
('sent', 'My Website', 675.0)
>>> order.order_line.mapped(lambda l: (l.product_id.display_name, l.price_unit))
[('[FURN_0096] Customizable Desk (White, Steel)', 675.0), ('[Delivery_007] Standard delivery', 0.0)]
>>> order.transaction_ids.mapped(lambda t: (t.reference, t.state, t.provider_id.name))
[('S00047', 'pending', 'Wire Transfer')]
```

`state` reads `sent`, not `sale`, exactly as the Concepts section predicted: Wire
Transfer can't confirm itself, a human has to see the money land and validate the
transaction by hand (Invoicing → Customers → Payments, or directly on the
transaction record) before this becomes a confirmed sale. The order line, the
delivery line, the linked transaction, all of it is the ordinary `sale.order` shape
chapter 22 already taught you to read.
</Step>

### Open a till and sell something

Point of Sale > pick a shop (the demo data ships Furniture, Clothes and Bakery) >
**New Session**. The Opening Control screen asks for a starting cash float:

- **Opening cash:** `150.00`

Click **Open Register**. This lands you in the actual POS terminal, the offline SPA:

![The POS terminal for Bakery Shop with an Apple Pie and a Wholemeal loaf in the cart, tax 2.25, total 17.24, and a numeric keypad plus Payment button below](/screens/ch42-pos-terminal-cart.jpg)

Tap two products, then **Payment**, choose **Cash**, and **Validate**. The receipt
that comes back is a real one, not a mockup:

```text
Ticket 261-3-000001
1  Apple Pie                              $ 13.80
1  Wholemeal loaf                         $  3.44
Subtotal                                  $ 14.99
Tax 15%                                   $  2.25
```

Start a **New Order**, ring up something else, and pay by **Card** this time instead
of Cash. Two orders, two different payment methods, is what makes the next part
worth doing.
</Step>

<Step>
### Close the register, and hit the gotcha on purpose

Open the hamburger menu (top right) and choose **Close Register**. The Closing
Register screen shows what each payment method expects against what you told it you
counted:

![The Closing Register screen showing Cash expected 167.24, opening 150.00, payments in cash 17.24, but Counted 0.00 and Difference -167.24 in red](/screens/ch42-pos-closing-discrepancy.jpg)

**Counted reads $0.00 and Difference reads -$167.24, in red.** Nobody stole anything;
the Cash Count field simply defaults to `0`, not to the amount the drawer should
hold, and the screen takes that literally. This is worth doing once with real numbers
in front of you: leave a self-service closing screen at its default and you generate
a shortfall report for the full opening float plus every cash sale of the day, for a
till that is not actually short a cent.

Type the real total into **Cash Count** (`167.24`, the opening float plus your one
cash sale) and the **Card Count** already shows `16.10` from the card payment. Every
Difference line reads `$0.00`. Click **Close Register**.
</Step>

<Step>
### Read the journal entries it posted

Closing the session did not create one journal entry per order. It created three,
each doing one job:

```console
$ docker compose exec -T odoo odoo shell -c /etc/odoo/odoo.conf -d functional --no-http
>>> s = env["pos.session"].search([("config_id.name", "=", "Bakery Shop")], order="id desc", limit=1)
>>> s.move_id.name
'POSS/2026/08/0003'
>>> for l in s.move_id.line_ids:
...     print(l.account_id.code, l.account_id.name, l.debit, l.credit)
251000 Tax Received 0.0 4.35
400000 Product Sales 0.0 28.99
101300 Account Receivable (PoS) 16.1 0.0
101300 Account Receivable (PoS) 17.24 0.0
```

That first entry, `POSS/...`, is the session's sales summary: total revenue, total
tax, and one receivable line per order, all of it consolidated across every order in
the session rather than posted order by order. Then, one move per payment method,
clearing that receivable into the account each method actually settles to:

```console
>>> env["account.move"].search([("ref", "like", "Bakery Shop/00001")]).mapped("name")
['POSS/2026/08/0003', 'PBNK1/2026/00003', 'CSH3/2026/00001']
```

`PBNK1/...` ("Combine Card POS payments") moves the card receivable into an
Outstanding Receipts account, the same holding account chapter 28's invoice payments
used before bank reconciliation. `CSH3/...` moves the cash receivable straight into
the shop's own cash account, because a physical drawer needs no bank statement to
reconcile against; you just counted it. Three small, single-purpose entries instead
of one large one is the same "smallest correct unit" instinct chapter 27's manual
journal entries taught, applied by the framework itself this time.
</Step>

<Step>
### A look at the snippet library

Any website page, **Edit** in the top right, opens the same builder every Odoo site
uses:

![The website editor's Blocks panel: categories Intro, Columns, Content, Images, People, Text, Contact & Forms, Social, Catalog, Blogs, Events, and an Inner Content section with Text, Button, Image, Video, Separator, Accordion, Alert, Rating and Card entries](/screens/ch42-website-snippet-panel.jpg)

Each tile in that panel is one of the 191 `website.s_*` templates from Concepts.
Dragging one onto a page and editing its text or image is the entire mechanism; there
is no code to write for the large majority of a marketing site.

Which makes this the place to state the rule this chapter has been circling, the one to
carry out of Part 7:

- The page needs to **look** like something: a hero image, a pricing table, content
  arranged and restyled. Drag snippets. A component here redoes work Odoo already shipped.
- The page needs to **know** something: live data from your own models, a stateful widget,
  anything beyond arranging and restyling content. Write a component, chapters 39 to 41's
  territory.

Try dragging one yourself, this chapter's screenshot stops at showing you the panel; the
drag-and-drop itself is the snippet exercise below.
</Step>

</Steps>

## Gotchas

- **A website order paid by Wire Transfer stays `sent`, not `sale`.** Manual payment
  methods can't confirm themselves; the order sits at Quotation Sent until someone
  validates the transaction by hand once the money actually arrives. Don't read
  "unconfirmed" as "broken."
- **The Cash Count field defaults to `0`, not to the expected total.** Closing a
  register without entering a real count reports a shortfall for the entire opening
  float plus every cash sale, on a till that lost nothing.
- **Backend OWL components do not run on the website.** `web.assets_backend` (chapters
  39-41) and `web.assets_frontend` (the public site) are different bundles; nothing
  you built in this part appears there automatically.
- **A session posts one consolidated sales entry, not one per order.** Look for a
  journal entry named after every individual sale and you will not find one; the
  session itself is the unit of accounting.
- **Cash and card payments settle through different accounts.** Cash lands directly in
  the shop's cash account (you counted it yourself); card and other electronic
  methods land in Outstanding Receipts pending bank reconciliation, chapter 28's
  pattern again.
- **A snippet's copied arch means editing one page never touches another.** This is a
  feature, not a bug, but it also means a wording fix made on one page has to be
  repeated on every other page that dragged in the same snippet, since there is no
  shared instance to edit once.

## Exercises

1. ⭐ Place a second website order, this time adding the Conference Chair or
   Warranty cross-sell offered in the upsell dialog, and confirm both end up as
   separate `order_line` records on the same `sale.order`.
2. ⭐ Open a second POS session on a different shop (Clothes or Furniture), sell one
   order, and correctly close the register on the first attempt, using the numbers the
   Closing Register screen itself shows you rather than guessing.
3. ⭐⭐ Read `POSS/.../0003`'s tax line, `251000 Tax Received`, against the two
   orders' totals and confirm by hand that 15% of the untaxed subtotal produces the
   exact tax figure posted.
4. ⭐⭐ Find the `pos.payment.method` record backing the "Customer Account" payment
   option and explain, from its `journal_id` (or lack of one), why paying by customer
   account does not need a settlement entry the way Card does.
5. ⭐⭐⭐ Actually drag a snippet onto a page in the website editor (Edit > any block
   under Intro or Content), fill in real text and an image, and Save. Confirm from the
   shell that the page's `ir.ui.view.arch_db` now contains your edited copy of the
   snippet's markup, not a reference to the original template.
6. ⭐⭐⭐ Break-it lab: open a POS session, ring up an order, and before paying, open
   devtools and go offline (Network tab > Offline). Complete the sale anyway, then go
   back online. Read what the POS SPA's IndexedDB-backed order queue does once
   connectivity returns, and explain why this would have been meaningfully harder to
   build as a normal client action like chapter 41's dashboard.
