**Goal:** build the demo database Parts 4 and 5 run in, then learn the partner, product
and unit models well enough that sales, purchase, inventory and accounting all make
sense. · **Time:** ~2 h · **Checkpoint:** none, this part runs in its own demo database

## Why this matters

For thirteen chapters you have been building LibreFleet as an island. Your
`librefleet.part` carries its own `standard_cost` and `list_price`, your service orders
compute their own totals, and nothing you wrote touches a single core app. That was the
right way to learn the ORM: no core surface area in the way.

Now the bill arrives. Odoo already ships all of it, and better than your version does.
Part 6 is going to ask you to "extend the Sales flow" and to bridge `librefleet.part` to
`product.product`, and neither instruction means anything until you know what the Sales
flow is and what a product actually is in this system.

So this part changes the exercise. Instead of writing code, you run a business in the
interface and then read what it did to the database. That second half is the part no
functional training does, and it is the only reason a developer should care about the
first half. By the end of Part 5 you will have watched a quotation become an order, a
delivery, an invoice, a payment and a pair of balanced journal lines, and you will have
seen every table it touched on the way.

Three models sit under all of it. Get these right and the next nine chapters are mostly
consequences.

## Concepts

### The functional database, and why it is not your tutorial one

Everything in Parts 4 and 5 happens in a **separate database with demo data**. Three
reasons, all practical:

- Functional exploration is miserable without demo data. You want a chart of accounts,
  products, customers and a few historic orders to look at, not an empty shell.
- Installing Sales, Purchase, Inventory and Accounting into your `tutorial` database
  would change every checkpoint diff from here on, and Part 6 still needs that database
  clean.
- Odoo 19 installs **without** demo data unless you ask, and that flag only works on a
  fresh database. Chapter 34 explains exactly why an upgrade cannot add it later.

`odoolings` already takes `--db`, so nothing new is needed to check your work here.

### One model wears four hats: `res.partner`

Most systems have a customers table and a suppliers table. Odoo has neither. It has
**one** `res.partner` model, and the distinctions you would expect to be types are
fields on it:

- **Company or person**: `is_company`, a boolean. Companies can have people under them
  through `parent_id`, and read them back through `child_ids`.
- **Customer, vendor, or both**: not a type at all, but two <Term k="rank (`customer_rank` / `supplier_rank`)">counters</Term>, `customer_rank`
  and `supplier_rank`. They rise as a partner appears on orders. A partner with both
  above zero is a company you buy from *and* sell to, which is completely normal, and
  which a two-table design makes awkward.

This is why chapter 12 had you point `librefleet.vehicle.owner_id` at `res.partner`
rather than inventing an owner model. You were already using the spine.

### One product, two models: `product.template` and `product.product`

This one confuses every new Odoo developer, and it is worth slowing down for.

- <Term k="product template / product variant">`product.template`</Term> is the product as a human talks about it: "Brake Pad Set".
- `product.product` is the specific thing you can put in a box: "Brake Pad Set
  (Front)". Odoo calls these **variants**.

Add an attribute to a template with two values and Odoo generates one `product.product`
per combination, by itself, on save. Everything that moves through stock, gets sold, or
gets costed is a `product.product`. Everything a person names and prices is usually a
`product.template`.

<Mermaid

    P2["product.product<br/>Brake Pad Set (Rear)"]
    T --> A

A template with no attributes still gets exactly one variant. There is no such thing as
a `product.template` you can sell directly, which is why so much core code takes
`product_id` (a variant) even when the form you were looking at was a template.

The field split follows the same logic. `name` is stored on the template, and
`product.product.name` is a *related* field reading `product_tmpl_id.name`, which you
can confirm yourself in the Hands-on. So renaming a template renames every variant,
because there was only ever one name.

### Product types changed in Odoo 19

If you have seen older Odoo, unlearn this one. The type field now offers exactly three
choices:

| `type` | Label in the interface |
|---|---|
| `consu` | Goods |
| `service` | Service |
| `combo` | Combo |

A **Combo** isn't a product you stock or deliver, it's a bundle: it points at one or
more `product.combo` records, each offering the customer a choice of items ("pick a
sandwich, pick a drink") at one prorated price. LibreFleet never needs one; it exists
here only so you recognise the word when you see it.

There is **no `product` type any more**. The old "storable product" idea is now a
separate boolean, <Term k='is_storable ("Track Inventory")'>`is_storable`</Term>, labelled **Track Inventory**, and it is contributed by
the Inventory app rather than by `product`. So "is this a physical thing" and "do we
count it in stock" became two independent questions, which is the better model: a
consumable you buy and immediately expense is Goods without inventory tracking.

<Callout type="info" title="On Odoo 18 this differs">
  Odoo 18 and earlier used `type = 'product'` to mean "storable", alongside `consu` and
  `service`, with no `is_storable` field and no `combo`. Any tutorial, forum answer or
  module written against 18 or older that branches on `type == 'product'` needs
  rewriting for 19. This is exactly the kind of change chapter 47's migration work is
  made of.
</Callout>

### Units of measure are a tree in Odoo 19

Units also changed, and more deeply than the product type did. A <Term k="unit of measure (`uom.uom`)">`uom.uom`</Term> record now
declares its size **relative to another unit**:

- `relative_uom_id` points at the unit it is defined against (Dozens points at Units).
- `relative_factor` says how many of that unit it contains (12).
- `factor` is the absolute quantity, computed recursively up the chain and stored.
- `parent_path` indexes the resulting hierarchy.

Older Odoo grouped units into a category with one reference unit each and a `uom_type`
of bigger, smaller or reference. **Both `category_id` and `uom_type` are gone in 19.**
Conversion still works the same way from the outside: ask a unit to convert a quantity
into another unit and it walks the chain.

### The habit worth building today: which model is this screen?

Every functional chapter from here on ends with you reading the database, and that is
impossible until you can answer "what model am I even looking at?" for any screen in
Odoo. Two ways:

- **In the interface**, turn on developer mode (chapter 4 covered where the toggle
  lives) and the bug menu in the top bar names the model behind the current view and
  lets you inspect its fields.
- **In the shell**, which is the one you can automate: `env["ir.model"]` is a table of
  every model, and `fields_get` on any model returns its fields with their types and
  labels. The Hands-on uses this, and so does every check `odoolings` runs.

## Hands-on

This chapter's Hands-on has two movements, and every chapter in Parts 4 and 5 follows
the same shape. First you **run the flow** like a user. Then you **read what it did**,
which is where the learning is.

### Movement 1: run the flow

#### 1. Build the functional database

Once, and reused by chapters 22 to 30:

```console
$ docker compose exec odoo odoo -c /etc/odoo/odoo.conf -d functional \
    -i sale_management,purchase,stock,mrp,crm --with-demo --stop-after-init
...
INFO functional odoo.modules.loading: Modules loaded.
```

That installs six apps, because `account` arrives as a dependency of Sales:

```console
$ docker compose exec db psql -U odoo -d functional -t -c \
  "select string_agg(name,', ' order by name) from ir_module_module where state='installed' and name in ('sale_management','purchase','stock','mrp','crm','account');"
 account, crm, mrp, purchase, sale_management, stock
```

Restart the web server so it picks up the new database, then log in at
[http://localhost:8069](http://localhost:8069) and choose `functional`. Your `tutorial`
database is untouched, and stays that way for the whole of Parts 4 and 5.

#### 2. Take a snapshot before you touch anything

This is the tool that makes the second movement possible. It records the current state
of the models these two parts care about, so you can ask what changed afterwards:

```console
$ python3 odoolings.py snapshot --db functional
snapshot: 15 models, 705 records (database 'functional')

Now do something in the interface, then: odoolings.py diff --db functional
```

#### 3. Create a product with variants, in the interface

Go to **Sales → Products → Products → New** and build a brake pad set:

1. **Product Name**: `Brake Pad Set` (exactly, so the check can find it).
2. **Product Type**: `Goods`. Note there is no "storable" option to pick.
3. Tick **Track Inventory**. That is the storable behaviour, as its own field.
4. **Sales Price**: `79.00`.
5. On the **Attributes & Variants** tab, add an attribute named `Axle` with values
   `Front` and `Rear`. Leave its variant creation mode as "Instantly".
6. Save.

Now look at the top of the form: a **Variants** smart button appeared, reading 2. You
created one product and Odoo made two.

![The Brake Pad Set product form: a Variants smart button reading 2, Product Type set to Goods, and Sales Price $79.00](/screens/ch21-brake-pad-set-variants.jpg)

### Movement 2: read what it did

#### 4. Ask the database what that save actually changed

```console
$ python3 odoolings.py diff --db functional
product.product     +2
      new   Brake Pad Set (Front)    lst_price=79.0
      new   Brake Pad Set (Rear)     lst_price=79.0
product.template    +1
      new   Brake Pad Set            list_price=79.0  type=consu
```

Read that carefully, because it is the whole concept in four lines. **One** form save
created **three** records: the template you named, and two variants you never typed. The
attribute values became products. This is what people mean when they say Odoo "generates
variants", and now you have watched it happen instead of reading about it.

#### 5. Confirm the relationship from the shell

```console
$ docker compose exec odoo odoo shell -c /etc/odoo/odoo.conf -d functional --no-http
```

```python
>>> t = env["product.template"].search([("name", "=", "Brake Pad Set")])
>>> t.product_variant_count
2
>>> t.product_variant_ids.mapped("display_name")
['Brake Pad Set (Front)', 'Brake Pad Set (Rear)']
```

Then prove where the name lives, rather than taking the Concepts section's word for it:

```python
>>> env["product.template"]._fields["name"].store
True
>>> env["product.product"]._fields["name"].related
'product_tmpl_id.name'
```

Stored on the template, *related* on the variant. Exactly the mechanism chapter 13
taught you, used by core on the model you will meet most often.

#### 6. See both tables in Postgres

```console
$ docker compose exec db psql -U odoo -d functional -c "
select t.id as tmpl_id, t.name->>'en_US' as template, p.id as product_id, p.default_code
from product_template t join product_product p on p.product_tmpl_id = t.id
where t.name->>'en_US' = 'Brake Pad Set' order by p.id;"
 tmpl_id |   template    | product_id | default_code
---------+---------------+------------+--------------
      51 | Brake Pad Set |         69 |
      51 | Brake Pad Set |         70 |
(2 rows)
```

Two rows, one `tmpl_id`. Your ids will differ from these. The `name->>'en_US'` is not a
typo: translatable fields are stored as JSON, so a plain `where name = '...'` finds
nothing. Worth remembering the first time a psql query on a name comes back empty.

#### 7. Look at the partner model wearing all four hats

```python
>>> P = env["res.partner"]
>>> P.search_count([("is_company", "=", True)])
12
>>> P.search_count([("customer_rank", ">", 0)])
4
>>> P.search_count([("supplier_rank", ">", 0)])
2
>>> P.search_count([("customer_rank", ">", 0), ("supplier_rank", ">", 0)])
0
```

Twelve companies, four customers, two vendors, and in a fresh demo database no overlap
yet. That last number is the interesting one: nothing structural prevents it, and it
climbs the moment you buy from someone you also sell to. Confirm the hierarchy too:

```python
>>> c = P.search([("is_company", "=", True), ("child_ids", "!=", False)], limit=1)
>>> c.name, c.is_company, c.parent_id.name
('Acme Corporation', True, False)
>>> c.child_ids[:3].mapped("name")
['Addison Olson', 'Douglas Fletcher', 'Floyd Steward']
```

One model, one table, a company and its people.

#### 8. Walk the unit tree and convert something

```python
>>> unit = env.ref("uom.product_uom_unit")
>>> dozen = env.ref("uom.product_uom_dozen")
>>> dozen.relative_uom_id.name
'Units'
>>> dozen.relative_factor
12.0
>>> unit._compute_quantity(12, dozen)
1.0
>>> dozen._compute_quantity(1, unit)
12.0
```

Dozens is defined as twelve Units, and conversion walks that link in both directions. If
you go looking for the old category field to confirm the grouping, you will not find it:

```python
>>> "category_id" in env["uom.uom"]._fields
False
>>> "uom_type" in env["uom.uom"]._fields
False
```

## Gotchas

- **`product_variant_count` is computed and not stored, so you cannot search on it.** A
  domain like `[("product_variant_count", ">", 1)]` does not return an empty set, it
  raises `ValueError: Cannot convert product.template.product_variant_count to SQL
  because it is not stored`. Filter in Python instead:
  `templates.filtered(lambda t: t.product_variant_count > 1)`. Chapter 13's stored
  versus unstored distinction, met in the wild.
- **There is no `product` product type in Odoo 19.** Code written for 18 or earlier
  branches on `type == 'product'` to mean storable. In 19 that comparison is always
  false, and silently so. The storable question is `is_storable`.
- **`is_storable` comes from the Inventory app, not from `product`.** Without `stock`
  installed the field does not exist at all, so a module that reads it must depend on
  `stock` or guard for its absence.
- **A translatable field is JSON in Postgres.** `where name = 'Brake Pad Set'` matches
  nothing on `product_template`; you need `name->>'en_US'`. This catches everyone once,
  usually while debugging a query that "should" return a row.
- **Almost everything downstream wants a variant, not a template.** When core code asks
  for `product_id` it means `product.product`. Passing a template id where a variant is
  expected fails in confusing ways, because both are integers and neither the ORM nor
  Postgres can tell you which kind you meant.

   `Ceramic`, and predict the variant count before you save. Run
   `odoolings.py diff --db functional` afterwards and check whether your prediction and
   the diff agree. Then explain why an attribute with only one value does not multiply
   anything (the demo database's `Acoustic Bloc Screens` is exactly that case).
2. ⭐⭐ Find, from the shell, every template in the demo database with more than one
   variant. The obvious domain raises an error, so you will need the first Gotcha. Then

   `attribute_line_ids`.
3. ⭐⭐ Ticket-shaped: a colleague says "our supplier Azure Interior should not be
   showing up in the customer list". Using only `res.partner` fields, work out how Odoo
   decides who appears in each list, then describe what would have to be true of that
   record for the complaint to be valid. Change nothing, just diagnose it, which is most
   of what a support ticket actually needs.
4. ⭐⭐⭐ Break-it lab: create a unit called `Half Dozen` with `relative_uom_id` set to

   explain the number from the chain rather than from arithmetic you did in your head.
   Now try setting its `relative_uom_id` to itself and read what happens. What does that
