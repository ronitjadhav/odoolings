**Goal:** change an OCA module's behaviour for your project without touching its code,
and know what that choice costs you later. · **Time:** ~2.5 h · **Checkpoint:**
`code/checkpoints/ch51` (the new module only, LibreFleet is untouched this chapter).

## Why this matters

Chapter 49 left a real finding on the table. `sale_fixed_discount` converts a fixed
amount into a percentage and does not cap it, so a discount of 150.00 on a unit price of
100.00 gives a subtotal of -100.00 and a discount of 150%, with no error.

Now you have to do something about it, and this is the moment most developers get
wrong. The instinct is to open the file and fix it, because the file is right there in
`oca/sale-workflow/` and the fix is four lines. **Do that and you have created a
problem your successor will spend a day on:** the next `git pull` on that repository
either wipes your fix or refuses to merge, and nothing anywhere records that your
production behaviour is not the behaviour of the module you appear to be running.

There are three honest options and one dishonest one. This chapter walks the decision,
then builds the option you will pick most often, then shows you the bill that arrives
with it a year later.

## Concepts

### Three options, and how to choose between them

The question that decides it is not technical. It is **whose problem is this?**

<CardGrid cols={2}>
  <Card tone="sage" icon={<Icon name="branch" />} title="Upstream pull request">
    The behaviour is wrong for everybody, not just you. Fix it in the module, chapter
    45's mechanics, and every other user gets the fix. Slower, and it is not yours to
    schedule.
  </Card>
  <Card tone="sand" icon={<Icon name="tool" />} title="A module of your own">
    The behaviour is a policy question and reasonable projects would answer it
    differently. Add your answer in a module that depends on theirs, and leave theirs
    alone. Fast, yours to schedule, and it is on you forever.
  </Card>
  <Card tone="violet" icon={<Icon name="source" />} title="Fork the repository">
    You need changes so deep that gluing cannot express them. Real, occasionally
    correct, and expensive: you now migrate that fork every October, alone.
  </Card>
  <Card tone="plain" icon={<Icon name="fail" />} title="Edit the clone in place">
    The dishonest one. Invisible, unrecorded, and destroyed by the next pull. Never,
    including "just for now".
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

For today's finding, apply the test. Is an uncapped fixed discount wrong for everybody?
Not obviously: a credit line with a negative subtotal is exactly what some businesses
want, and the module's author may well have relied on it. It is a policy question, so it
gets a module of our own. **A behaviour you would have to argue about in a pull request
is usually a behaviour that belongs in your own module.**

### The glue module, which OCA already showed you

You built one of these in chapter 46 without calling it that, and chapter 50 watched OCA
install one automatically: `sale_order_secondary_unit` exists purely to connect
`product_secondary_unit` to `sale`. OCA calls that a **bridge module**, and the shape is
always the same:

- It depends on the module it changes, so it loads after it.
- It uses a classic extension, `_inherit` with no `_name` (chapter 31), to reach the
  same model.
- It contains only the difference. Nothing is copied.

<Mermaid

### Naming it, and why the name matters more here

Chapter 44's naming rule was about avoiding collisions in a global namespace. For a
glue module there is a second job: the name has to say what it glues. OCA's convention
is `<module you extend>_<what you add>`, so `sale_fixed_discount_limit` reads as "the
limit part of sale_fixed_discount" to anyone who lists the addons directory.

That is not decoration. Six months from now, someone debugging a rejected discount will
grep the installed module list for `discount`, and the name is the entire clue.

### The one method name you must not reuse

A classic extension puts your class in the same MRO as theirs, which means **a method
you define with a name they already used replaces theirs** unless you call `super()`.

`sale_fixed_discount` already has a constraint method called `_check_discounts`. Define
your own `_check_discounts` in your glue module and their consistency check quietly
stops running, and nothing warns you: same name, same model, last module loaded wins.
Give yours a name of its own and both run.

<Callout type="warn" title="Gotcha">
  This is the most expensive mistake in glue-module work, because it makes something
  *stop happening* rather than start. Before naming any method in an extension, grep
  the module you are extending for that name. `grep -rn "def _check" oca/sale-workflow/sale_fixed_discount/`
  takes two seconds.
</Callout>

```python title="addons/sale_fixed_discount_limit/__manifest__.py"
{
    "name": "Sale Fixed Discount Limit",
    "summary": "Refuse a fixed discount larger than the line's unit price",
```

One dependency, and it is the OCA module rather than `sale`. That is deliberate: you
need `discount_fixed` to exist before your constraint can mention it, and depending on
the module that defines it is what guarantees load order. Depending on `sale` instead
would leave you extending a field that may not be there.

Notice what the manifest does *not* have: no `data`, no views, no security. This module
adds one Python constraint, so it needs nothing else.

### Write the difference, and nothing else

```python title="addons/sale_fixed_discount_limit/models/sale_order_line.py"
from odoo import api, models
from odoo.exceptions import ValidationError


class SaleOrderLine(models.Model):
    _inherit = "sale.order.line"

    @api.constrains("discount_fixed", "price_unit")
    def _check_discount_fixed_not_above_unit_price(self):
        """A fixed discount above the unit price makes the line negative.

        sale_fixed_discount converts the amount to a percentage and does not
        cap it, which is a deliberate choice upstream. This is our policy, so
        it lives here rather than in a fork of theirs.
        """
        for line in self:
            if line.discount_fixed > line.price_unit:
                raise ValidationError(
                    self.env._(
                        "A fixed discount of %(discount)s is larger than the "
                        "unit price of %(price)s, which would make the line "
                        "negative.",
                        discount=line.discount_fixed,
                        price=line.price_unit,
                    )
                )
```

Fourteen lines of logic, and the docstring is doing real work: it records *why* this is
not a pull request. Write that down while you still remember, because the next person to
read this file will otherwise assume you did not know about upstream.

The rest is chapter-by-chapter familiar: `@api.constrains` from chapter 14,
`self.env._()` for the translatable message from chapter 44's linter finding, and a
`for line in self` loop because a constraint gets a recordset, not a record.

Wire up the imports:

```python title="addons/sale_fixed_discount_limit/__init__.py"

```


### Test your own rule, and keep the test out of the way of other modules

A glue module needs a test more than most code does, for a reason worth stating:
**it is the only place that records what you decided.** The constraint is four lines and
somebody will eventually wonder whether it was intentional.

```python title="addons/sale_fixed_discount_limit/tests/test_discount_limit.py"
from odoo.exceptions import ValidationError
from odoo.tests.common import TransactionCase


class TestDiscountLimit(TransactionCase):
    """The guard, on a line built from a product that already exists.

    Creating a product.template here instead would make the test depend on
    every other module's opinion of what a product template requires: in a
    database with website_sale installed, that create needs a column this test
    would never think to set. Reusing a product keeps the test about our own
    constraint.
    """

    @classmethod
    def setUpClass(cls):
        super().setUpClass()
        cls.product = cls.env["product.product"].search(
            [("sale_ok", "=", True)], limit=1
        )
        cls.order = cls.env["sale.order"].create(
            {"partner_id": cls.env["res.partner"].search([], limit=1).id}
        )

    def _line(self, discount_fixed):
        return self.env["sale.order.line"].create(
            {
                "order_id": self.order.id,
                "product_id": self.product.id,
                "product_uom_qty": 2,
                "price_unit": 100.0,
                "discount_fixed": discount_fixed,
            }
        )

    def test_discount_below_unit_price_is_allowed(self):
        self.assertEqual(self._line(25.0).price_subtotal, 150.0)

    def test_discount_equal_to_unit_price_is_allowed(self):
        self.assertEqual(self._line(100.0).price_subtotal, 0.0)

    def test_discount_above_unit_price_is_refused(self):
        with self.assertRaises(ValidationError):
            self._line(150.0)
```

```python title="addons/sale_fixed_discount_limit/tests/__init__.py"
from . import test_discount_limit
```

That docstring is the lesson chapter 49 half-taught. There, the OCA module's own tests
errored in your `functional` database because their `setUpClass` creates a
`product.template`, and `website_sale` (installed back in chapter 42) has a required
column that create never fills. **A test that creates a product template inherits every
installed module's opinion of what a product template needs.** Reusing an existing
product avoids the whole class of problem, and it is why the run below is green in the
same database that could not run theirs:

```console
$ docker compose exec odoo odoo -c /etc/odoo/odoo.conf -d functional \
    -u sale_fixed_discount_limit --test-tags /sale_fixed_discount_limit \
    --http-port=8070 --stop-after-init
```

```text

INFO functional odoo.addons.sale_fixed_discount_limit.tests.test_discount_limit: Starting TestDiscountLimit.test_discount_below_unit_price_is_allowed ...
```

Thirty-five commits on this branch touch that module. Scroll further and one of them is
worth reading twice:

```text
2024-05-25  [FIX] sale_fixed_discount: convert _onchange_discount_fixed() to computed method
```

An onchange became a compute. Any glue module that had overridden
`_onchange_discount_fixed` kept loading fine and quietly stopped doing anything, because
the method it was overriding no longer ran. **The thing you extend is a moving target,
and the failure mode is silence.** Your test suite is what turns that silence into a red
build, which is the real reason chapter 38's habit matters more on glue code than
anywhere else.

Then look at the date on the 19.0 migration: **2025-11-25**, a month or so after Odoo 19
itself shipped. That is the other half of the bill. Your module cannot be installed on a
new major until theirs is ported, so you have inherited their schedule. Chapter 47's
count is the honest planning number: roughly half the modules on the 18.0 branches had
reached 19.0 by the time this chapter was written.

That is not an argument against glue modules. It is the argument for knowing exactly how
many you have, which is a question you should be able to answer about any project you
work on:

The third check is worth a moment. A `ValidationError` raised from `@api.constrains`
aborts the whole write, so the line comes back untouched. Had you put the guard in an
overridden `write()` that saved first and checked afterwards, the value would already be
in the row when the error was raised, and check three is what tells those two designs
apart.

Nothing in these three can prove you did not shadow `_check_discounts`, which is why
this chapter's fourth exercise makes you do it on purpose and watch what happens.

## Gotchas

- **Never edit the clone in `oca/`.** The next `git pull` decides what happens to your
  change, and nothing anywhere records that it existed. `git -C oca/<repo> status` should
  always come back empty.
- **Depend on the module you extend, not on its dependencies.** `depends:
  ["sale_fixed_discount"]` is what guarantees `discount_fixed` exists when your
  constraint loads. Depending on `sale` would leave the load order to chance.
- **A method name you share with the module you extend replaces theirs.** Grep before
  you name, and remember the symptom is something silently *not* happening.
- **A test that creates core records inherits every installed module's requirements.**
  The OCA module's own tests cannot run in your `functional` database for exactly this
  reason. Build fixtures from records that already exist where you can.
- **Your glue module pins you to their migration schedule.** It cannot be installed on
  a new major before theirs is ported, and half the OCA modules take more than a year.
  Count your glue modules before promising an upgrade date.

## Exercises

1. ⭐ Add the second item from the module's own `ROADMAP.md`: the same guard on
   `account.move.line`, where `account_invoice_fixed_discount` puts its version of the
   field. Note that the field is `monetary` there and `float` on the sale line, and
   decide whether that changes your constraint.
2. ⭐⭐ Take the opposite decision, on paper. Write the pull request description you
   would open against `OCA/sale-workflow` arguing that the cap belongs upstream: what it
   changes, who it could break, and what you would offer as a compromise (a setting? a
   company field?). Chapter 45's etiquette section is the model.
3. ⭐⭐ Point a glue module at your own capstone instead. Write
   `librefleet_service_order_audit`, depending on `librefleet` and `auditlog`, that
   ships a confirmed `auditlog.rule` on `librefleet.service.order` as a data file. You
   now own a bridge between two modules you did not write together.
4. ⭐⭐⭐ Break-it lab, and it is the one from the Gotchas. Rename your constraint method
   to `_check_discounts`, upgrade, and then set `discount_fixed` and `discount` to values
   that contradict each other, which the OCA module's own constraint exists to reject.
   Watch it be accepted. Then explain, in writing, why nothing warned you, and put the
   name back.
